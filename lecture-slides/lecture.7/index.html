Cities Are Processes, Not Just Objects

  • Cities are built over time.
  • Urban form records decisions, infrastructure, migration, investment, and land use.
  • The same city can grow in one area while changing or declining in another.

A City Is More Than Its Downtown

  • The city is part of a larger settlement system.
  • Downtown is only one condition within a metropolitan landscape.
  • The “city” extends through multiple kinds of territory.

Urbanity Is a Spectrum

  • Real places often sit between categories; a place can ‘feel’ like one category while existing in another.

How Do We Recognize Urbanity?

  • Five guiding themes:

Signs of an Urban Core

  • Continuous building fabric
  • Mixed land uses
  • Fine-grained streets and blocks
  • Transit concentration
  • Limited open land
  • High activity and nighttime-light intensity

Signs of Suburban and Exurban Landscapes

  • Suburban: Lower-rise residential fabric > separated land uses > moderate density
  • Exurban: Dispersed development > large lots

Density Can Look Different in Different Places

  • The same density can produce different urban forms.
  • A 1 km cell may include parks, water, industrial land, or mixed development.
  • Density is a starting point for interpretation, not a final classification.

13 — Urban Area vs Metropolitan Area

Urban Area

  • Built-up settlement pattern

Metropolitan Area

  • Regional economic and commuting system.
  • New York City extends far beyond the five boroughs.

Census Urban and Rural Classifications

  • Census classifications are useful for comparing places.
  • Urban and rural are statistical designations.
  • They do not fully describe lived experience or urban character.
  • Use Census geography for U.S. study areas only.
