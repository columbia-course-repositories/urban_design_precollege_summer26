Observation Organization Exercise

Duration: 20–30 minutes

Objective

You have already completed your field observations at the High Line and Hudson Yards. Each of you has collected sketches, notes, photographs, and personal observations. The purpose of this exercise is to organize those individual observations into a shared body of evidence that will guide your group’s final project.

Today is not about drawing conclusions. It is about understanding what your group observed, identifying common themes, and organizing your evidence before moving into analysis and design.

Step 1 — Share Your Field Observations (8 minutes)

Each team member should take 2–3 minutes to present the material they collected during the field visit.

Share:

  • Observation notes
  • Urban sketches
  • Annotated photographs
  • Movement observations
  • Sightline sketches
  • Public life observations

As each person shares, the rest of the group should listen carefully and look for recurring ideas or observations.

At this stage, avoid interpreting the site or debating whether observations are “correct.” Simply share what you documented.

Step 2 — Identify Common Themes (7 minutes)

As a group, discuss the observations you have just shared.

Consider the following questions:

  • What did everyone notice?
  • Which observations appeared more than once?
  • Where do your observations differ?
  • What surprised you?
  • What evidence seems most important?

Begin grouping similar observations together. Look for patterns rather than isolated moments.

Step 3 — Organize Your Evidence (10 minutes)

Working together, organize your material into the following categories.

Observation Notes

What written observations best describe your study area?

Urban Sketches

Which sketches communicate important spatial relationships or experiences?

Annotated Photography

Which photographs provide evidence rather than simply documenting the site?

Movement Studies

What patterns of circulation, gathering, or pedestrian movement did your group observe?

Sightline Observations

Which views help explain the organization of the site?

Public Life Observations

How did people occupy and use the space?

As you organize your material, begin identifying the strongest examples within each category.

Step 4 — Identify Missing Information (5 minutes)

No field study captures everything.

Discuss what additional information your group may still need to complete the project.

Possible examples include:

  • GIS mapping
  • Historical imagery
  • Land use information
  • Demographic data
  • Additional diagrams
  • Precedent research

Record these as research tasks that can be completed during the next phase of the project.

Step 5 — Build Your Evidence Table (5 minutes)

Spread all of your team’s material across the table.

Include:

  • Sketchbooks
  • Observation notes
  • Printed maps
  • Photographs
  • Annotated drawings
  • Any additional research already collected

Arrange the material so that it begins to tell the story of your observation territory.

As a group, ask yourselves:

If someone who had never visited Hudson Yards walked up to this table, what story would our evidence tell?

Look for:

  • Missing information
  • Repeated observations
  • Contradictory evidence
  • Relationships between sketches, photographs, notes, and maps

This is your first opportunity to curate your work as a team rather than as individual observers.

Deliverable

By the end of today’s exercise, your group should have:

  • Shared all individual field observations
  • Identified common themes
  • Organized evidence into common categories
  • Identified missing information and future research tasks
  • Created a physical “evidence table” that begins to communicate the story of your observation territory

Remember:

The goal today is not to decide whether Hudson Yards and the High Line are successful.

The goal is to organize your collective evidence so that your analysis, drawings, GIS maps, physical model, and final presentation are all grounded in careful observation.
