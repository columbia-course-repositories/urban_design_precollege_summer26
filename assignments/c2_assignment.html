🧭 Assignment 2: Mapping a Vantage Point

Location, Orientation, and Urban Representation

Date: Tuesday, June 30, 2026
AM Session: 11:10 a.m.–1:00 p.m.
PM Session: 3:10–5:00 p.m.
Location: Columbia University Campus and Surrounding Study Area

In class 1, we explored the area surrounding Columbia University through direct observation, field notes, and the practice of the dérive. By slowing down and paying attention to everyday urban conditions, you began to identify relationships between streets, buildings, public spaces, and the people who use them.

This assignment builds upon those observations by introducing the concept of a vantage point — a specific location from which urban space can be documented and represented. Urban designers must not only observe places, but also communicate what they see through maps, diagrams, sketches, photographs, and other forms of visual representation. Learning how to establish a location, record a viewing direction, and document spatial conditions are foundational skills in urban analysis and design.

Through this exercise, you will select and document a single observation point, record its location and orientation, and create an urban sketch that captures the structure and character of the surrounding environment. The goal is not artistic perfection. Rather, the sketch should serve as a tool for careful observation, spatial understanding, and communication.

As Jan Gehl argues in Life Between Buildings (Class 2 Homework Reading), cities are experienced from the human perspective and at the scale of everyday life. This assignment asks you to slow down, look carefully, and consider how urban space is perceived from a specific place and point of view.

NoteKey Idea

A map tells us where we are.
A compass tells us which direction we face.
A sketch helps us communicate what we observe.

📍 Exercise 1: Establishing a Vantage Point

Purpose

This exercise asks you to establish a fixed observation point before beginning your sketch. By recording your position, orientation, and field of view, you create a spatial record that can be revisited, compared, and analyzed.

Materials

  • Printed study-area map provided by the instructor
  • Sketchbook or notebook
  • Pen or pencil
  • Phone with map / GPS / compass access
  • Optional phone camera

Part 1: Select a Vantage Point

Choose one observation location within the approved study area.

You may either:

  • Return to the same location you documented during Assignment 1, allowing you to revisit and represent a place you have already observed; or
  • Select a new location that offers a different perspective on the relationship between Columbia University and the surrounding neighborhood.

There is no correct choice. The goal is to establish a fixed observation point that provides a meaningful view of urban space.

After selecting your location, remain there for the duration of the sketching exercise.

Part 2: Mark Your Location

On your printed map:

  1. Place a clear dot marking where you are standing.
  2. Label the nearest street, intersection, or landmark.
  3. Draw an arrow showing the direction you are facing.

Part 5: Define Your Field of View

On your printed map, draw a simple field-of-view diagram.

Include:

This does not need to be precise. It should simply show where you are standing and what portion of the urban environment you are observing.

✏️ Exercise 2: Urban Sketching and Spatial Documentation

Purpose

This exercise introduces urban sketching as a method of spatial documentation. Your sketch should communicate the structure and experience of the place from your chosen vantage point.

Keep in mind, we have a specific purpose - observe carefully and represent clearly. We don’t need to create masterpieces; we are sketching, focused on the drawing conventions we have discussed together in class prior to today’s fieldwork.

Part 1: Observe Before Drawing

Spend 3–5 minutes looking carefully before you begin to sketch.

During this time:

  • Do not begin drawing immediately.
  • Do not focus on details first.
  • Notice the overall structure of the place.

Ask yourself:

  • What are the largest forms in view?
  • Where are the edges?
  • Where is the street?
  • Where is open space?
  • Where does movement occur?
  • What feels important to include?

Part 2: Sketch the Spatial Structure

Begin your sketch with broad strokes.

Focus first on:

Built Form

  • Building masses
  • Street walls
  • Major edges
  • Entrances or thresholds
  • Skyline or rooflines

Public Realm

  • Sidewalks
  • Streets
  • Plazas
  • Open spaces
  • Seating areas

Environmental Elements

  • Trees
  • Plantings
  • Shade
  • Sunlight
  • Topography or elevation changes

Avoid excessive detail. You do not need to draw every window, brick, sign, or person.

Tip

Avoid excessive detail. You do not need to draw every window, brick, sign, or person.

Part 3: Add Activity as a Secondary Layer

While People and movement are important, they should not dominate the drawing.

Add simple marks or notes showing:

  • Pedestrian movement
  • Bicycles
  • Vehicles
  • Waiting or gathering areas
  • Places of activity or inactivity

Think of activity as a layer of information that helps explain the space.

Part 4: Annotate Your Sketch

Add labels, arrows, and short notes directly onto your sketch.

Annotations may identify:

  • Movement patterns
  • Boundaries and edges
  • Transitions between campus and city
  • Public and private conditions
  • Places that feel open or closed
  • Sounds, activity, or atmosphere
  • Landmarks or orientation points
Note

If you use annotations, they should help someone else understand what you noticed and why it matters.

Part 5: Optional Photo Documentation

You may take 1–5 reference photographs to support your work.

Photographs may document:

  • Your vantage point
  • Your field of view
  • Important spatial conditions
  • Details that support your sketch or notes

Do not photograph people closely or intrusively. If people are visible, they should appear only as part of the broader public environment.

Important

Do not photograph people closely or intrusively. If people are visible, they should appear only as part of the broader public environment.

Reflection

Answer the following questions in your sketchbook or notebook:

  1. What did sketching reveal that a photograph might not?

  2. What was most difficult to represent?

  3. How did recording your location and direction change the way you understood the place?

📚 Class 2 Readings

Required Reading A

Jan Gehl. Life Between Buildings: Using Public Space (selected excerpt, 2011). Read 1. Life Between Buildings - pages 8 - 30

  • LINK to PDF

As you read, focus on Gehl’s argument that cities are experienced from the human perspective and at the scale of everyday life. Consider how streets, buildings, public spaces, and patterns of movement influence the way people perceive and experience urban environments.

For our next class discussion, think about how digital technologies—particularly mobile phones, social media, and navigation applications — may change the way people observe and engage with urban space. Do these technologies enhance our understanding of cities, or do they reduce our awareness of the environments around us?

Note

While the required reading is Part 1: Life Between Buildings, feel free to read further but keep in mind the more narrow assigned section is our assignment objective.

Class 2 Homework Assignment

📖 Reading Reflection

As you complete the readings, keep in mind the following guiding questions:

  1. Why does Gehl argue that cities should be understood from the perspective of everyday users rather than only through plans, maps, or aerial views?

  2. How does experiencing a place in person differ from viewing the same place through a photograph, map, or digital screen?

  3. What aspects of urban space become noticeable only when we slow down and observe carefully from a single location?

  4. To what extent do mobile phones, social media, and digital technologies change the way people experience and engage with urban environments?

Be prepared to discuss your responses during the opening discussion of Class 3, Wednesday, July 1st.

NoteKey Takeaway

Urban designers use location, orientation, observation, and representation to better understand places. A successful sketch does not need to be beautiful; it needs to help us see and explain urban space more clearly.
