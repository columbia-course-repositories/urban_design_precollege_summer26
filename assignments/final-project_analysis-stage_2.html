Analysis Workshop

Duration: 30–45 minutes

Objective

Your group has now organized its field observations and identified a common research theme.

The purpose of today’s workshop is to transform your observations into analysis.

Urban designers do more than document a place—they interpret it. Your task is to identify patterns, relationships, and spatial systems that explain how your observation territory functions.

By the end of today’s workshop, your group should have a clear analytical direction that will guide the remainder of the project.

Step 1 — Refine Your Research Question (5 minutes)

Begin by writing a single research question based on your group’s assigned theme.

Examples:

  • Infrastructure Across Time — How has infrastructure shaped the evolution of this place?
  • Materiality of the High Line — How do material choices preserve the identity of the former railway?
  • Connectivity & Use — How does the High Line connect people to Hudson Yards and the surrounding city?
  • Accessibility — How accessible is the High Line for different users and modes of movement?

Keep your question focused. It should guide every diagram, map, sketch, and model that follows.

Step 2 — Identify Spatial Relationships (10 minutes)

Review your evidence and begin looking for patterns and relationships.

Discuss:

  • What relationships appear repeatedly?
  • What patterns emerge?
  • What is connected?
  • What is separated?
  • What seems intentional?
  • What seems accidental?

Rather than describing individual objects, begin describing systems.

Step 3 — Determine Your Analytical Drawings (15 minutes)

Decide which analytical drawings will best communicate your research question.

Possible outputs include:

Spatial Diagrams

Illustrate relationships between buildings, landscape, infrastructure, and public space.

Movement Diagrams

Show pedestrian movement, gathering spaces, circulation routes, and areas of congestion.

GIS Context Maps

Develop maps that place your observation territory within its broader urban context.

Possible GIS layers include:

  • Land use
  • Building footprints
  • Parks and open space
  • Transit
  • Demographics
  • Flood risk
  • Historical infrastructure

Building / Open Space Relationships

Study how built form shapes public space.

Behavioral Observations

Translate your field notes into patterns of use.

Spatial Organization

Illustrate paths, nodes, edges, districts, landmarks, thresholds, and spatial sequences.

Step 4 — Curate Your Evidence (10 minutes)

Return to your field observations and decide what best supports your research question.

Ask yourselves:

  • Which sketches best communicate our ideas?
  • Which photographs provide the strongest evidence?
  • Which GIS maps will strengthen our analysis?
  • Which observations should be emphasized?
  • What information is still missing?

Remember:

Not everything needs to appear in the final presentation.

Select the strongest evidence.

Step 5 — Organize Team Responsibilities (5 minutes)

Decide who will lead each component of the project.

Possible responsibilities include:

  • Spatial diagrams
  • GIS mapping
  • Sketch refinement
  • Photography selection
  • Physical model
  • Written analysis
  • Final InDesign board layout

Every team member should have a clearly defined role moving forward.

Step 6 — Develop a Working Hypothesis (5 minutes)

Based on your evidence and analysis, write one or two sentences that summarize your current interpretation of your observation territory.

Begin with the following prompt:

Based on our observations, we believe…

Support your statement by identifying 3–5 pieces of evidence that reinforce your thinking.

Remember that this is a working hypothesis, not a final conclusion. Your interpretation may evolve as your GIS maps, physical model, diagrams, and final presentation develop.

Deliverable

By the end of today’s workshop, every group should have:

  • A clearly defined research question
  • A list of required analytical drawings
  • A GIS mapping strategy
  • A curated collection of supporting evidence
  • Clearly assigned team responsibilities
  • A written working hypothesis beginning with:

Based on our observations, we believe…

supported by 3–5 pieces of evidence.
