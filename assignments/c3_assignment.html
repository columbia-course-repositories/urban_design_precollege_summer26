Assignment Overview

In Assignment 1, you explored the city through observation, movement, and field notes. In Assignment 2, you documented a specific urban location through a vantage point, coordinates, orientation, sketching, and annotation.

Assignment 3 asks you to transform that observed location into spatial data and place it within a larger geographic framework. Using GeoJSON.io and QGIS, you will create a digital observation point, explore basemaps, and produce a PDF map that connects street-level experience to map-based representation.

This assignment introduces Geographic Information Systems (GIS) as a tool for urban design analysis. The goal is not simply to learn software. The goal is to understand how an observed place can become a mapped location within a larger urban context.

NoteKey Idea

A sketch records what you experienced at a place.
A coordinate records where that place exists.
A map helps connect that place to a larger urban context.

Learning Objectives

By completing this assignment, students will:

  • Convert latitude and longitude coordinates into a digital geographic feature.
  • Understand how observations can be represented as spatial data.
  • Create a GeoJSON point from a recorded observation location.
  • Import spatial data into QGIS.
  • Explore basemaps using the QuickMapServices plugin.
  • Produce a PDF map that communicates an observation location.
  • Reflect on the relationship between street-level observation and map-based representation.

🌐 Exercise 2: Creating a GeoJSON Point

Purpose

This exercise transforms your recorded observation location into a digital geographic feature. GeoJSON is a common spatial data format used to store geographic information such as points, lines, and polygons.

In this exercise, your observation location becomes a point.

Step 1: Open GeoJSON.io

Go to:

https://geojson.io

Step 2: Locate Your Observation Point

Use the search bar or manually navigate to your observation location.

You may use:

  • Your latitude and longitude coordinates
  • A nearby address or intersection
  • A recognizable landmark

Step 3: Select the Point Tool

Select the Point Tool from the toolbar.

Click once at your observation location.

A point should appear on the map.

Step 4: Add Attributes

In the right-hand panel, add the following properties to your point.

Attribute Example
name Observation Point
location Broadway & 116th Street
latitude 40.8075
longitude -73.9626
observation Assignment 2
Note

Use your own location and coordinates; the information above is for example purposes only.

Step 5: Verify Your Point

Make sure your point appears in the correct location.

Check:

  • Does the point match your Assignment 2 observation location?
  • Are the latitude and longitude values correct?
  • Are the attributes complete?

Step 6: Save Your GeoJSON File

Save your file as:

lastname_observation.geojson

This is a working file for the assignment. You do not need to submit the GeoJSON file unless instructed, but you will need it in QGIS.

NoteObservation → Data → Map

Your sketch records what you experienced at a place.
Your GeoJSON point records where that place exists.
Together they connect urban experience and spatial data.

🧭 Exercise 3: Importing Your Data into QGIS

Purpose

This exercise introduces QGIS as a tool for viewing, organizing, and representing spatial data. You will import your GeoJSON point and begin building a map around your observation location.

Step 1: Open QGIS

Open QGIS and create a new project.

Note

Prior to our workshop, we will review QGIS installation and troubleshooting for a sucessful install.

Step 2: Add Your GeoJSON File

Navigate to:

Layer → Add Layer → Add Vector Layer

Browse to your file:

lastname_observation.geojson

Click Add.

Step 3: Confirm Your Observation Point

Your observation point should appear in the QGIS map canvas.

If it does not appear:

  • Check that the GeoJSON file saved correctly.
  • Right-click the layer and select Zoom to Layer.
  • Ask for instructor support.

Step 4: Open the Attribute Table

Right-click your observation point layer and open the attribute table.

Confirm that the table includes:

  • name
  • location
  • latitude
  • longitude
  • observation

🧩 Exercise 4: Installing QuickMapServices

Purpose

Basemaps provide geographic context. They allow you to compare your observation point across different map representations, such as street maps and satellite imagery.

Step 1: Open Plugin Manager

In QGIS, navigate to:

Plugins → Manage and Install Plugins

Step 2: Search for QuickMapServices

Search for:

QuickMapServices

Step 3: Install the Plugin

Select QuickMapServices and click:

Install Plugin

Step 4: Confirm Installation

Close the Plugin Manager.

Confirm that the following menu appears:

Web → QuickMapServices

🛰️ Exercise 5: Exploring Basemaps

Purpose

This exercise asks you to compare how different basemaps represent the same observation location. Each basemap reveals certain urban conditions while hiding others.

Add OpenStreetMap

Navigate to:

Web → QuickMapServices → OSM → OpenStreetMap

OpenStreetMap may help you identify:

  • Streets
  • Buildings
  • Public spaces
  • Labels
  • Pathways

Add ESRI World Imagery

Navigate to:

Web → QuickMapServices → Esri → Esri World Imagery

Satellite imagery may help you identify:

  • Building footprints
  • Tree canopy
  • Open space
  • Roof surfaces
  • Larger spatial patterns

Compare Basemaps

Turn layers on and off.

Zoom between:

  • Campus scale
  • Neighborhood scale
  • City scale

Consider:

  • What does each basemap reveal?
  • What does each basemap hide?
  • Which basemap best supports your observation?
  • How does map scale change your understanding of the location?

✍️ Reading Reflection

Required Reading A

James Corner. The Agency of Mapping (Chapter 1.12 in the PDF).

  • LINK to PDF
Note

Note

As you read, focus on Corner’s argument that maps are not neutral records of reality. Maps select, organize, emphasize, and sometimes omit information. They can describe existing conditions, but they can also help designers discover patterns, relationships, and future possibilities.

For our next class discussion, think about how mapping changes the way we understand a place. Consider how your own observation point changes when it moves from a sketch and field note into a point on a map.

Reading Themes

Maps Are Not Neutral

  • Maps do not simply reproduce reality. They select, organize, and communicate information about places.

Mapping as Interpretation

  • Every map reflects choices about what to include, emphasize, or omit.

Mapping as Discovery

  • Mapping can uncover patterns, connections, and relationships that are difficult to observe from a single vantage point.

Mapping as Design

  • Maps are not only tools for documentation. They can help designers imagine new possibilities for cities and landscapes.

Observation and Representation

  • Mapping is one of many ways to represent place, alongside observation, photography, sketching, and writing.

Guiding Questions

Respond briefly to the following questions.

  1. Corner argues that maps are not neutral representations of reality. What choices do mapmakers make, and how do those choices influence what we see?

  2. How is a map similar to a sketch?

    (Consider how both maps and sketches involve selection, simplification, and emphasis rather than recording everything equally.)

  3. What kinds of urban information can maps reveal that may be difficult to observe directly from the street?

    (Think about scale, movement, infrastructure, land use, and spatial relationships.)

  4. Corner suggests that mapping can be a creative and imaginative act. In what ways can maps help designers think about the future rather than simply document the present?

  5. How might mapping your observation point change your understanding of the place you observed?

    (What new questions become visible when you shift from a street-level perspective to a map-based perspective?)
