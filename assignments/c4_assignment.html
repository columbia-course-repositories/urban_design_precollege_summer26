Assignment Overview

In previous classes, you observed, documented, and mapped a location in New York City. You recorded its position, created a map, and explored its geographic context using GIS.

In this assignment, you will become an urban investigator.

Cities are constantly changing. Buildings are constructed and demolished, infrastructure expands, neighborhoods evolve, and land uses shift over time. At the same time, some elements of the city persist for decades or even centuries. Streets remain in place, parks endure, and planning decisions continue to shape the city long after they are made.

Using historical aerial imagery, historical maps, and GIS, you will investigate how your observation point and surrounding area have changed through time. Your goal is to identify evidence of urban change and urban persistence, and to use that evidence to better understand the city that exists today.

NoteKey Question

What evidence from the past helps explain the city that exists today?

Learning Objectives

By completing this assignment, students will be able to:

  • Compare historical and contemporary representations of urban space.
  • Interpret historical aerial imagery and historical maps.
  • Use GIS as a tool for investigating urban change.
  • Identify patterns of continuity and transformation in urban environments.
  • Communicate findings through maps, imagery, and written reflection.

📍 Exercise 1, Part II: HOLC Mapping Investigation

Historical maps can reveal more than physical change. They can also reveal how cities were understood, classified, and governed in the past. During this guided investigation, we will examine historical HOLC (Home Owners’ Loan Corporation) maps and neighborhood descriptions to explore how historical planning and lending practices shaped urban development.

As a class, we will investigate how these historical designations may continue to influence contemporary neighborhoods and urban conditions.

Resources

  • HOLC Mapping Inequality Project
  • HOLC NYC - Manhattan Spatial Resource #1
  • HOLC NYC - Manhattan Spatial Resource #2

Tasks

  1. Locate your observation point or study area within the HOLC map.
  2. Identify the HOLC designation assigned to the area.
  3. Review the historical neighborhood description.
  4. Compare historical classifications with present-day conditions.
  5. Discuss observations as a class.

Consider

  • How was this neighborhood described historically?
  • What assumptions or values shaped these classifications?
  • What physical evidence of change can be observed today?
  • What physical features or patterns appear to persist?
  • How might historical decisions continue to shape contemporary urban environments?
  • What can historical maps reveal that may not be visible in contemporary maps or aerial imagery?

🌐 Exercise 2: Historical Map Investigation

Historical aerial imagery allows us to see how cities have changed from above. Historical maps provide a different kind of evidence. Rather than simply recording what existed at a particular moment, maps often reflect the priorities, technologies, planning ideas, and spatial understanding of their time.

In this exercise, you will use a historical GeoTIFF map and QGIS to investigate your observation point and surrounding area. By comparing a historical map with a contemporary basemap, you will look for evidence of urban change and urban persistence. Streets, parks, infrastructure, waterfronts, neighborhoods, and other urban features may appear differently across time, revealing how the city has evolved.

Your goal is not simply to compare two maps. Your goal is to use historical evidence to investigate the relationship between the past city and the city that exists today.

In the section Historical Maps (Georeferenced Geotiffs) located at the end of the assignment, you will select at least one historical map for the list of 14 historical map options.

Tasks

  1. Select at least one historical map from the Historical GeoTIFF Collection.
  2. Download the GeoTIFF file. Note that some of these files are approaching 1gb, so expect some to take a few minutes to download fully.
  3. Load the historical map into QGIS.
  4. Add a contemporary basemap for comparison.
  5. Locate your observation point.
  6. Compare the historical city and the contemporary city.

Consider

  • Which urban features remained?
  • Which urban features disappeared?
  • What new features emerged?
  • What does the historical map reveal that aerial imagery does not?
  • What evidence of urban change can be observed?
Note

Prior to working on Exercise 2 on your own, we will review QGIS processes for loading GeoTIFF files and the comparative process using a basemap.
