Urban Planning and Design: Class 10 Assignment #8 - Climate Risk + Resiliency - NYC

Columbia University | Pre-College Programs | Urban Planning and Design | Summer 2026

Published

July 12, 2026

🎯 Learning Objectives

By the end of this lesson, students will be able to:

  • Explain why the Lower East Side is particularly vulnerable to coastal flooding.
  • Interpret flood hazard maps and relate them to urban form.
  • Compare the historical landscape with the contemporary city.
  • Recognize that coastal resiliency is a global urban design challenge.
  • Describe how the ESCR project responds to environmental conditions.

❓ Guiding Question

Why is the East Side Coastal Resiliency Project located here, and what can maps tell us that is difficult to see on the ground?

🗺️ Data & Mapping Resources

Today’s lesson combines several public mapping resources used by planners, designers, engineers, and researchers. Together, these resources allow us to explore the relationship between historic landscapes, modern urban development, and future flood risk.

🌿 Welikia Project

The Welikia Project is an interactive web map that reconstructs Manhattan’s original landscape prior to European settlement.

We will use Welikia to compare today’s city with:

  • Historic shoreline
  • Wetlands
  • Streams
  • Natural topography

Because Welikia is browser-based, it serves as a historical reference rather than a downloadable GIS dataset.

🌊 NYC Flood Risk GeoJSON

For our GIS analysis we will use vector flood hazard polygons compiled from New York City and New York State sources.

These GeoJSON layers can be loaded directly into QGIS and overlaid with other spatial datasets.

These layers allow us to investigate:

  • Flood hazard areas
  • Flood exposure
  • Neighborhood vulnerability
  • Spatial relationships between flood risk and urban development

🏙️ NYC Waterfront Revitalization Program

The Waterfront Revitalization Program provides an interactive planning map describing New York City’s waterfront planning initiatives.

We will use this resource to better understand:

  • Waterfront planning
  • Public access
  • Coastal resiliency
  • Existing city projects

🌍 Climate Central Coastal Risk Screening Tool

Finally, we will expand beyond New York City to examine how coastal flooding affects cities around the world.

This interactive global viewer allows us to compare flood vulnerability across hundreds of coastal cities.

👀 Part 1 — Reflect on the Site Visit (5 min)

Before opening any maps, discuss the field visit.

Questions

  • What did you notice?
  • Where did you encounter water?
  • What seemed unusually engineered?
  • What surprised you?

Organize observations into four categories:

  • 💧 Water
  • 🌳 Landscape
  • 🏗 Infrastructure
  • 🚶 Public Space

🌊 Part 2 — Reading Flood Risk (10 min)

GIS Layers

Display progressively:

  • Manhattan basemap
  • ESCR project boundary
  • Flood hazard polygons
  • FEMA / NYC flood zones
  • (Optional) Sea level rise projections

Discussion Questions

  • What do these flood zones represent?
  • Which neighborhoods appear most vulnerable?
  • Why is Midtown largely outside these zones?
  • Why does flood risk concentrate along the Lower East Side?
  • If another Hurricane Sandy occurred today, where would water likely enter first?

💡 Key Idea

Flood maps represent probability and exposure, not certainty.

Urban planners use these maps to anticipate future risk and guide long-term investment.

🌿 Part 3 — Looking Back: The Original Landscape (8 min)

Open the Welikia map.

Compare:

  • Historic shoreline
  • Wetlands
  • Streams
  • Today’s Manhattan

Discussion Questions

  • What existed here before the modern city?
  • Which landscapes disappeared?
  • Which natural systems remain hidden beneath today’s streets?
  • Do today’s flood-prone areas resemble yesterday’s wetlands?

💡 Big Idea

The city did not erase the original landscape.

It built over it.

Flooding often reveals that underlying geography.

🔄 Part 4 — Connecting the Layers (7 min)

Overlay mentally (or digitally):

Historic Landscape

↓

Flood Hazard

↓

Today’s Neighborhood

↓

ESCR Project

Discussion Questions

  • What patterns appear?
  • Is flooding random?
  • Why is the resiliency project located here?
  • Why wasn’t a wall simply built at the shoreline?

Introduce several design constraints:

  • Neighborhoods
  • East River Park
  • FDR Drive
  • Utilities
  • Public access
  • Ecology
  • Recreation

💡 Big Idea

The ESCR project is much more than a flood wall.

It redesigns the relationship between the city and the waterfront.

🌍 Part 5 — Coastal Cities Around the World (10 min)

Open the Climate Central Coastal Risk Screening Tool.

Compare several cities:

  • 🗽 New York
  • 🌴 Miami
  • 🇳🇱 Amsterdam
  • 🇮🇹 Venice
  • 🇬🇧 London
  • 🇮🇩 Jakarta
  • 🇹🇭 Bangkok
  • 🇨🇳 Shanghai

Discussion Questions

For each city:

  • Why is it vulnerable?
  • What infrastructure is being built?
  • How is the response similar to ESCR?
  • How is it different?

💡 Big Idea

New York is one example of a much larger global movement toward coastal adaptation.

Cities around the world are redesigning their waterfronts in response to sea level rise, storm surge, and increasingly frequent coastal flooding.

💬 Wrap-Up Discussion (5 min)

Return to the guiding question.

Why is the East Side Coastal Resiliency Project located where it is?

Support your answer using evidence from:

  • 👀 Field observations
  • 🌊 Flood maps
  • 🌿 Historic landscapes
  • 🌍 Global comparisons

✅ Key Takeaways

Students should understand that:

  • 🌊 Flooding follows geography as much as weather.
  • 🗺️ Maps reveal relationships that are difficult to observe in the field.
  • 🌿 Historic landscapes continue to shape modern cities.
  • 🏗 Coastal resiliency combines engineering, ecology, and public space.
  • 🌍 Cities around the world are adapting to similar environmental challenges.
